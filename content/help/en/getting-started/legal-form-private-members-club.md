A **private members' club** is a club run by and for its members, with no
charitable status and (usually) no intention of public benefit reporting.
Many bowls clubs sit here, especially older ones with a long-established
clubhouse and bar.

Legally, you're typically an **unincorporated association** or sometimes a
**limited company by guarantee** under the surface — but operationally and
culturally you're a private club.

## How members' clubs make money

- **Membership subscriptions** — your bedrock.
- **Bar and catering income** — profitable for many clubs; subject to
  alcohol licensing rules.
- **Function-room hire** — weddings, wakes, parties.
- **Visiting team fees** — green fees from external matches.
- **Sponsorship** — local businesses, often in exchange for clubhouse signage
  or scorecard branding.
- **Brewery support** — your beer supplier may grant or loan towards
  refurbishments in return for an exclusivity deal.
- **Member loans / debentures** — a more formal way to raise capital from
  members for a major project.
- **Fundraising events** — quizzes, raffles, prize draws (within Gambling
  Commission rules for the larger ones).

## What you can NOT easily access

- **Charitable trust grants** — most are charity-only.
- **Gift Aid** — not available without charity / CASC status.
- **Mandatory rates relief** — discretionary only.
- **Public-benefit-required funders** — Lottery, Sport England prefer
  constituted-and-open-to-community structures.

## Should you consider CASC status?

If you'd like the tax reliefs without becoming a charity, look at
**Community Amateur Sports Club (CASC)** registration with HMRC. You stay a
private members' club operationally, but unlock 80% rates relief and Gift
Aid. See the CASC help article.

## Risks to be aware of

- **Personal liability** of committee members (if unincorporated).
- **Alcohol licensing** — premises licence holder is personally responsible.
- **Employment law** — paid staff need contracts, payroll, RTI.
- **Discrimination law** — you can be a private club, but you can't
  discriminate on protected characteristics in admissions.

## When this form makes sense

- Established club with a thriving bar and members willing to pay realistic
  subs.
- No appetite for charity-style governance reporting.
- Modest grant requirements.

## When to think about changing

If you're applying for material public funding, want to own a building
long-term, or your committee starts losing sleep over personal exposure,
look at **CASC** (lighter touch) or **CIO/SCIO** (full charity model).
