A **Community Interest Company (CIC)** is a limited company with two
extras: an **asset lock** (assets and profits stay within the community
purpose) and a **community interest statement** (you spell out who benefits
and how). Regulated by Companies House and the **Office of the Regulator of
Community Interest Companies**.

## Why a club might pick a CIC

- **Trades commercially** — well-suited if you run significant non-charitable
  trading (clubhouse with extensive third-party hire, café open to public).
- **Asset lock reassures funders** — your facility can't be sold off and
  pocketed by directors.
- **Lighter regulator** than the Charity Commission.

## Why a club might NOT pick a CIC

- **Can't access most charity-only grants** — many trusts and foundations
  exclude CICs.
- **No Gift Aid** on member donations.
- **No mandatory rate relief** — discretionary only.
- **Companies House filings** on top of the CIC regulator's annual report.

For most bowls clubs, **CIO/SCIO/CASC will be a better fit** unless you have
a specific commercial-trading reason to choose CIC.

## Funding routes a CIC unlocks

- **Power to Change** (England) — supports community businesses.
- **Social investment** — CICs can take repayable finance from Big Society
  Capital, Triodos, etc.
- **Community shares** (CIC limited by shares) — raise capital from the
  community with regulated dividend caps.
- **Local authority commissioning contracts** — CICs are often preferred for
  delivering paid public services.
- **Dormant Assets Scheme** funds (England) — increasingly opened to CICs.

## Two CIC types

- **CIC limited by guarantee** — like a charity structurally; no shares.
  Most clubs would pick this.
- **CIC limited by shares** — can raise community shares; useful if you want
  members to invest financially.

## Compliance

- **Annual community interest report** to the CIC Regulator.
- **Companies House annual accounts and confirmation statement.**
- **Asset lock** — can only transfer assets to another asset-locked body.
